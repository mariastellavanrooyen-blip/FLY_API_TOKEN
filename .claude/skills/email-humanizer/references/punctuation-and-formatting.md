# Punctuation and formatting tells

These are habits of *form*, not word choice — an email can avoid every
cliché phrase and still read as AI-written because of how it's punctuated
and laid out.

## 1. The em dash (—)

The single most-flagged tell. AI writing reaches for the em dash constantly
to bolt a clarifying or dramatic aside onto a sentence — like this one.
Humans mostly don't punctuate that way in everyday email.

**Fix:** for each em dash, pick one:
- Split into two sentences.
- Replace with a comma, if the aside is short.
- Replace with parentheses, if it's a genuine side note.
- Just delete it and the clause becomes unnecessary anyway (common).

Rule of thumb: a client email should have zero or one em dash. More than one
is a strong tell.

## 2. Rule-of-three lists

"Clear, concise, and effective." "Fast, reliable, and affordable." AI writing
defaults to three-item parallel lists constantly, even when two items (or a
plain sentence) would do.

**Fix:** cut the list down to the one or two words that actually matter, or
rewrite as a normal sentence.

## 3. "Not only X, but also Y"

A formal rhetorical construction that rarely appears in real email between
people who know each other.

**Fix:** "X, and also Y" or just two separate sentences.

## 4. Bullet-itis

Turning a two-sentence email into a bulleted list with a header ("Summary:",
"Key Points:", "Next Steps:") is a common AI reflex. It reads as a report,
not a message from a person.

**Fix:** if the email has fewer than ~4 distinct items, write it as prose.
Reserve bullets for genuinely long enumerations (5+ items) where a client
needs to scan.

## 5. Bold text for emphasis in running prose

Bolding **random phrases** mid-sentence to flag "importance" is an AI habit,
not a human email habit.

**Fix:** remove bold from prose. If something truly needs to stand out, say
it in its own short sentence instead of formatting it.

## 6. Colon-headers in a casual email

"Overview:", "Background:", "Next steps:" inside a short email to one client
makes it read like a status report generated by a tool.

**Fix:** drop the headers; let the paragraph breaks do the organizing.

## 7. Semicolon-joined "essay" sentences

Repeated use of semicolons to fuse two independent clauses into one long,
formal sentence ("The report is attached; please review it at your
convenience.").

**Fix:** just use a period. Two short sentences read more human than one
semicolon sentence.

## 8. Rhetorical-question openers

"Ever wondered how to save time on X?" as a hook before getting to the
point — common in AI-generated marketing copy, out of place in a 1:1 or
bulk client email.

**Fix:** open with the actual point.

## 9. Exclamation-point enthusiasm stacking

"Great news! We're thrilled to let you know! Thanks so much!" — multiple
exclamation points in a short email reads as performative.

**Fix:** use at most one exclamation point, only where it's earned.

## 10. Numbered "Step 1 / Step 2" framing for a simple ask

Turning "please send the signed form and pay the invoice" into a numbered
2-step list with bold labels is over-structuring a small request.

**Fix:** state it as one sentence unless there are genuinely many sequential
steps a client must follow in order.

## 11. Over-symmetrical paragraph lengths

AI drafts often produce paragraphs that are suspiciously equal in length,
each covering one "topic" tidily. Real email is lumpier — one line here, a
longer explanation there.

**Fix:** don't be afraid of a one-sentence paragraph next to a longer one.
