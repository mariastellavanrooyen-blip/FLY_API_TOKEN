# Over-descriptive / structural patterns that read as AI

These aren't individual words — they're habits of *content and structure*
that make an email feel over-produced even when no single sentence is
obviously a cliché.

## 1. Restating the question before answering it

"Great question — you're asking whether the invoice covers both services.
To answer that..." A person just answers: "Yes, it covers both."

**Fix:** answer first. Only restate context if the client is likely to have
forgotten what they asked.

## 2. Padding with backstory before the ask

Walking through the full history of a situation before getting to what you
actually need ("As you may recall, back in March we discussed... since then
there have been several developments... which brings us to...").

**Fix:** lead with the ask or the news. Add backstory only if it's needed to
act on the ask, and keep it to one sentence.

## 3. Hedging everything

"This might potentially cause a delay in some cases, depending on various
factors." Stacking multiple hedges on one claim is a strong AI tell — real
people commit to a statement.

**Fix:** pick one level of certainty and state it plainly: "This will likely
delay things by a few days" or "This will delay things by a few days,"
not both hedged and vague at once.

## 4. Over-explaining obvious things

Spelling out something the client already understands ("As you know, an
invoice is a document requesting payment for services rendered...").

**Fix:** cut explanations of things the recipient already knows given who
they are and the relationship.

## 5. Generic warmth with no specific content

"I hope you're having a wonderful week" or "I hope business is going well"
when there's no actual knowledge behind it reads as filler, because it is.

**Fix:** either reference something real and specific (a project, an event
you know they had, their actual situation), or skip the pleasantry line
entirely and go straight to the message.

## 6. Meta-commentary about the email itself

"I'm writing this email to update you on..." / "The purpose of this message
is to..." — describing the email instead of just being the email.

**Fix:** delete the meta-sentence; the content that follows already makes
the purpose obvious.

## 7. Summarizing what was just said

Ending a short email with a recap paragraph ("So to summarize, we've
covered X, Y, and Z above") when the email itself was three sentences long.

**Fix:** only summarize if the email was genuinely long (several distinct
topics); otherwise cut the recap.

## 8. Stacking multiple calls to action

Ending with three different asks/CTAs ("Let me know if you have questions!
Feel free to schedule a call! Looking forward to your reply!") makes the
close feel automated and leaves the client unsure what's actually wanted.

**Fix:** pick the one thing you actually want the client to do next and say
only that.

## 9. Adjective stacking on a product/service

"Our innovative, comprehensive, and robust solution" — three adjectives
doing the work that one concrete fact should do.

**Fix:** replace the adjectives with one specific, checkable detail (what it
actually does, a number, a date).

## 10. Uniform formality regardless of relationship

Using the same maximally-polished, formal register for a long-time client
you'd normally text-message-tone with, and for a brand-new prospect. Real
people vary their tone by relationship; AI drafts default to one "safe,"
professional register throughout.

**Fix:** before finalizing, check the register matches how you'd actually
talk to this specific client — adjust warmth/formality accordingly, and
don't be afraid of a short, casual line when it's genuinely a casual
relationship.

## 11. Perfectly balanced "on one hand / on the other hand" framing

Presenting every point as a neatly weighed pro/con even in a simple update
email, when the actual situation isn't actually two-sided.

**Fix:** just state the conclusion or recommendation; skip the false
balance.
